In-House Agency vs. Freelancer vs. No-Code Builder: How to Choose

By the Vantage Studio team 8 min read

Quick answer

It comes down to three questions: how many disciplines the project needs (design, code, strategy, or all three), how much support you'll want after launch, and how much accountability matters if something goes wrong. A freelancer suits one well-defined task. A no-code builder suits a simple site you're happy to maintain yourself. A full studio makes sense once the project spans multiple disciplines or the business genuinely depends on the outcome.

What each option actually is

A freelancer is one person, usually strong in one or two disciplines — design, or development, occasionally both. A no-code builder is a platform (Wix, Squarespace, Shopify, and similar) where you assemble the site yourself from templates, with no development team involved at all. A full-service studio is a small team covering design, development, and strategy together, with someone accountable for the whole outcome rather than just their own piece of it.

None of these is objectively "better" — they solve different problems. The mismatch happens when the project's actual complexity doesn't match the option chosen for it.

Cost and timeline: what really differs

Recent research on how small businesses actually build their sites found something worth sitting with: businesses are nearly evenly split between outsourcing to a web design agency and building in-house, at 45% and 46% respectively. Neither approach dominates, because neither is right for every business — the deciding factor is almost always project complexity, not budget alone.

Freelancer pricing tends to track hours worked, which is efficient for a narrow task and unpredictable for a broad one. No-code builders shift cost from a project fee to a recurring subscription, with your own time replacing a chunk of what you'd otherwise pay someone else to do. Studio pricing is scoped up front against a fixed process — see our own five-stage process for exactly what that looks like in practice — which trades a higher starting cost for a predictable one.

When a freelancer is the right call

A freelancer is the right call for a single, contained task with a clear finish line — a one-off landing page, a design refresh with no development attached, a focused fix to an existing site. The risk grows with scope: the moment a project needs both design and development handled well, you're either hiring two freelancers and coordinating them yourself, or asking one person to be equally strong at both, which is uncommon.

When a no-code builder is enough

If the site is genuinely simple — a handful of pages, standard functionality, no custom logic — and you're comfortable maintaining it yourself, a no-code builder is a legitimate, sensible choice. It's often the right answer for an early-stage business validating an idea before investing further. The signal that you've outgrown it is usually one of three things: fighting the templates more than using them, needing an integration the platform doesn't support, or genuine visitor complaints about speed.

When you need a full studio

A studio earns its higher starting cost once a project needs more than one discipline done well at the same time, needs to integrate with other business systems, or the business result depends directly on the site working — a lead-generation engine for a sales team, a booking system a hotel's revenue depends on, a patient portal a healthcare provider is accountable for. In these cases, the cost of getting it wrong is higher than the cost of the extra hands needed to get it right the first time.

How this looks at Vantage

We turn away work that's genuinely better suited to a freelancer or a no-code builder — a studio that only ever recommends its own biggest offering isn't giving independent advice.
